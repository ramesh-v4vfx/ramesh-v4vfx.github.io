HOW THE PHYSICS WORKS
  • Event horizon: rs = 2GM/c². Stars, white dwarfs and neutron stars are told apart by radius (neutron star ≲ 35 km, white dwarf ≲ 0.05 R☉).
  • Tidal disruption: the less compact object is torn apart if its tidal (Roche) radius, rt = R(Macc/M)1/3, is larger than the accretor’s horizon or surface. The Hills mass is the heaviest black hole that can still disrupt a given object outside its horizon. Roughly half the debris is assumed to be accreted.
  • Black hole mergers: radiated energy and final spin come from numerical-relativity fits for non-spinning, quasi-circular binaries; the ringdown frequency uses the dominant ℓ=m=2 quasi-normal mode.
  • Neutron stars: the remnant’s fate uses approximate mass thresholds around the maximum neutron-star mass (≈2.2–2.3 M☉). The real answer depends on the equation of state and spins, so treat those rows as estimates.
  • White dwarfs: a pair heavier than the Chandrasekhar mass (≈1.4 M☉) is treated as a Type Ia supernova.
  • Animation: stylized. Sizes and distances are not to scale, but the orbit decays the way a gravitational-wave inspiral does — slowly, then in a rapid plunge.

Set the mass and, for stars, the radius of two objects (black hole, neutron star, white dwarf or star), run the collision, and see the real physics outcome — black hole merger with gravitational-wave mass loss, tidal disruption event, kilonova, supernova or direct capture — rendered as a 3D inspiral animation.
